**Research focus:** linking electrolyte structure, interfacial charge transfer, and mass transport to stable metal deposition.

## The question

Aqueous zinc batteries offer opportunities for energy storage, but their performance depends on coupled processes at the metal–electrolyte interface. How can we distinguish intrinsic reaction kinetics from transport effects, and use that understanding to design more reversible interfaces?

## Published work

### 1. Electrolyte structure and interfacial stability

My work has investigated high-entropy, water-in-organic, and urea-modified electrolytes, connecting solvation and intermolecular interactions with water activity and zinc reversibility. Related studies examine how anions regulate the electric double layer and nucleation.

- [High-entropy electrolytes and multi-solvation structures](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202416619)
- [Chaotropic salt-aided water-in-organic electrolytes](https://doi.org/10.1002/adfm.202311271)
- [Anion-regulated electric double layers and progressive nucleation](https://doi.org/10.1002/adfm.202314002)

### 2. Intrinsic charge-transfer kinetics

I use mechanistic electrochemistry to examine the assumptions behind conventional kinetic measurements. A representative study combines ultramicroelectrode voltammetry and Marcus–Hush analysis to evaluate charge transfer in aqueous zinc systems.

- [Beyond conventional methods for evaluating charge-transfer kinetics](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsenergylett.5c02639)

### 3. Transport, deposition texture, and morphology

My research connects ion transport to zinc deposition behavior, including crystallographic texture. Work on molecular-crowding and water-content-gradient electrolytes explores how spatial and transport effects shape electrode performance.

- [Mass-transfer limitation and zinc deposition texture](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202407881)
- [Sandwich-structured electrolytes with a water-content gradient](https://doi.org/10.1002/cssc.202402737)

## Continuing direction

Facet-resolved electrochemical measurements provide a route to separating the roles of surface structure and electrolyte environment in metal plating and stripping. This direction builds on experience with scanning electrochemical cell microscopy (SECCM) and single-crystal zinc electrodes; not all ongoing work is represented by the papers above.
